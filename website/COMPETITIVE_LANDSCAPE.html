1The strategic wedge

Every competitor sells a walled garden. We sell the door between them.

The multi-room / network audio market today splits cleanly into three groups, each with a different lock-in strategy:

  • Walled-garden hardware vendors — Sonos, Bluesound, HEOS (Denon), MusicCast (Yamaha), BluOS — sell speakers AND the software, and their software refuses to drive anyone else's speakers. Buy in, you're in for life.
  • Apple's ecosystem — AirPlay 2 + HomePod + Apple Music multi-room. Works beautifully if every speaker is AP2-capable. Useless for the ~half of in-home speakers that aren't.
  • Audiophile single-protocol streamers — Roon, iFi, Bluesound Node — solve the protocol gap but cost $400–700+ per device and require buying Roon-Ready / BluOS-compatible speakers, which is its own lock-in.

Then there's a handful of open-source senders (Airfoil, AirParrot, AirConnect, shairport-sync) that cross protocols, but they're software-only, require a Mac/PC, have no Room Tuning, no HDMI ARC, no consumer hardware story.

What nobody sells: a single product that captures the TV / Mac / phone's audio, fans it out to AirPlay 1 + AirPlay 2 + Sonos + Chromecast + Bluetooth speakers simultaneously, in sync, with cross-protocol room tuning, at consumer prices. That's the wedge SuperAudio occupies — today's foundation is measurement-assisted sync with a manual by-ear tune; the fully hands-free version comes with AirPlay 2. Everyone else picks one ecosystem and defends it. We're explicitly the router between them.

Open-source senders / bridges

A 2026 sweep of GitHub for "the project that does what SuperAudio does." Findings: every well-maintained project is either a receiver, a one-direction bridge, or a media server. No project combines Mac system-audio capture with cross-protocol fan-out to existing AirPlay 1+2/Sonos/Cast speakers. This isn't an accident — the GPLv2/v3 receiver lineage (shairport-sync) hasn't been extended into a sender-side equivalent, and the cross-protocol bridges (AirConnect) are intentionally narrow-shape.

6Moat — what's structural, what's just first-mover

An advantage is only a moat if a competitor can't take it without breaking something more valuable. Here's the honest split.

The differentiation matrix above shows what we do that competitors don't. This section asks the harder question: which of those gaps are durable? Some are structural — incumbents would have to abandon their business model to copy them. Others are just first-mover advantages a well-funded company could erase in a quarter. Confusing the two leads to lazy pricing and lazy planning. Better to be honest now.

Half-life — what changes the picture

Naming the conditions under which the moat weakens, so we'd see it coming:

  • Apple opens AirPlay 2 to third-party software senders — currently MFi-gated, intentionally. If they relax, our "we're the only AP2 software path" claim disappears (we'd still own AP1 + Sonos + Cast, but the lossless renaissance pitch weakens).
  • Sonos ships a "play to AirPlay 2" mode — they could. They haven't because it cannibalizes Sonos-speaker sales. If they decide it's net-positive (e.g., to defend against Apple), the fan-out moat compresses on the Sonos-as-source vector — but not on Mac-as-source, which is our actual product.
  • Google adopts a cross-vendor multi-room standard — Matter-for-audio is rumored periodically. If shipped widely, the protocol-translation moat softens. Five-plus years out at minimum.
  • A well-funded startup raises against the same wedge — most likely vector. Defense is shipping speed and Room Tuning quality. Our head start began 2026-05-18 with shipped Mac-mic calibration measurement (chirp cross-correlation, cross-protocol); hands-free auto-apply lands with AP2 (M12), and the M11 iPhone-mic walk-around extends it. Cross-protocol calibration measurement is no longer hypothetical for us.
  • A vendor consortium ships an open cross-brand device-profile standard — would compress the crowdsourced-device-support moat to "we got there first with better tooling." Possible if Matter-for-audio (or similar) gains real traction; not imminent. Defense is shipping the Claude Skill onboarding flow and building enough profile coverage in the GitHub repo that switching to a vendor standard becomes the slower path.

None of these trigger conditions is imminent. The most credible threat is a competitor noticing the same wedge before our Room Tuning ships. The defense is shipping it.

7Patents — what to fear, what's free

Multi-room audio has real patent exposure. Most of it doesn't apply to us; some of it does. Here's the honest map.

HIGH RISK
Sonos multi-room sync patents

Sonos has aggressively patented multi-room audio synchronization since the mid-2000s. They sued Google in 2020 over multi-room patents (settled mostly in Sonos's favor on key claims). Their patent portfolio covers:

  • Specific algorithms for clock synchronization across speakers
  • Stereo pairing and grouping behaviors on their own hardware
  • Their proprietary SonosNet mesh networking
  • Specific UPnP/SOAP control-flow patterns
Our defense: we do not implement our own multi-room synchronization algorithm. We use each protocol's native sync mechanism — RAOP timing-channel for AirPlay, Sonos's own UPnP grouping for Sonos. We are a router between ecosystems, not an inventor of sync. Sonos's patents are about inventing sync; we're about using existing sync. Worth a formal patent attorney review before Hub Pro hardware mass-production (~$3–5K freedom-to-operate search).
MEDIUM RISK
Apple AirPlay 2 protocol

AirPlay 2 sending requires Apple's MFi (Made for iPhone/iPad) licensing program. Implementing an AirPlay 2 sender outside MFi has not been done publicly at scale; Apple has not enforced against open-source implementations (pyatv, etc.) for many years, but enforcement is theoretically possible.

Our defense: the planned AirPlay 2 sender (M12) is built on the MIT-licensed pair_ap pairing library and standard, publicly-documented protocols — the same posture as our AirPlay 1, Sonos, and Cast paths. AirPlay 2 receiving (which Apple TVs and HomePods do natively) stays on their hardware; our $5 AirPlay 2 addon sends to speakers that are already AP2-licensed receivers, using standard public discovery. We pair and stream over published protocols; we don't ship Apple's proprietary code.
LOW RISK
Apple AirPlay 1 / RAOP protocol

The original AirTunes / RAOP protocol was reverse-engineered publicly in 2008–2009. It's used verbatim in shairport-sync, libraop, Airfoil ($35 retail since 2008), AirConnect, OwnTone, node-airtunes, and dozens of other shipping products for 16+ years. Apple has never enforced against any of them.

Our defense: we use the publicly-documented protocol with the publicly-known Apple AirPort Express RSA key (a numerical constant, not copyrightable). Code is fresh-written Swift; we don't copy from GPL sources.
LOW RISK
Audio codec patents (ALAC, AAC)

Apple Lossless (ALAC) was open-sourced by Apple in 2011 under Apache 2.0. AAC-LC patents (Fraunhofer, Dolby) generally have ended or are covered by macOS's built-in AVFoundation encoder license.

Our defense: use Apple's open-source ALAC encoder + AVFoundation's built-in AAC-LC. Both are royalty-free for our use case.
LOW RISK
Chromecast / Google Cast protocol

Google has never enforced against Cast protocol reverse-engineering. node-castv2, OwnTone, and many open-source Cast senders ship without Google action.

Our defense: when we ship the Chromecast addon, fresh-write the protocol from publicly-documented references. Same posture as AP1.
LOW RISK
HDMI ARC / eARC audio extraction

HDMI Forum's HDMI specs are licensed; the ARC / eARC audio paths are part of standard HDMI implementations. Hundreds of products (HDMI audio extractors, AVRs, soundbars) implement ARC audio extraction.

Our defense: Hub Pro uses commodity HDMI audio extractor chipsets sourced from Asian ODMs that already pay HDMI licensing. We integrate them, we don't reverse-engineer them. Our HDMI implementer cert ride along with the chipset vendor.
Total patent-related budget before hardware ships: ~$3–5K for a freedom-to-operate search by a patent attorney specializing in audio / consumer electronics. Required before mass-production of any hardware SKU. Not required for Mac or iOS app releases — software through App Store / direct download is in well-trodden ground.

9The strategic wedge, restated

Why this competitive position is durable.

The conclusion
Every other player picks one ecosystem and defends the wall. We're the door.

Sonos, Bluesound, HEOS, MusicCast, BluOS, Apple's AirPlay 2 — all defending their own walled gardens. They will never bridge to competitors because their business model is selling speakers, not bridges.

Roon, Dirac, iFi — premium-priced, niche-focused. They serve the top 5% of audiophiles and have no interest in mass market.

Airfoil, AirParrot, AirConnect — software-only, no hardware story, no Room Tuning, premium pricing for what they do.

SuperAudio's wedge is the combination of: (a) cross-ecosystem fan-out at consumer prices, (b) Room Tuning that works across protocols, (c) HDMI ARC for the TV use case, and (d) a hardware path so non-Mac households are reachable. No competitor combines these four. The wedge is durable because the walled-garden incumbents cannot compete without abandoning their lock-in business model.
