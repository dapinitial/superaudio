1The magic moment

Three sentences of user experience that define the entire product line.

Day 1 — plug Hub Pro into your TV's HDMI ARC port, scan a QR code with your phone, pick which speakers you want, give it a tune.

Day 2 — press the TV power button. In two seconds, audio is playing on every speaker in the house, at the levels you saved, in sync. No phone touched. No app opened. Just the TV remote you already use.

Press volume on the same remote — volume changes on every speaker, balance preserved. Press mute — everything quiets. Power off — everything stops cleanly.

Nobody else does this. Sonos Beam controls only Sonos speakers via the TV remote. Apple TV controls only HomePods. WiiM bridges one source to one output. SuperAudio Hub Pro is the only product that turns a single TV remote into the universal volume control for a heterogeneous mesh of B&W AirPlay 1 + Sonos + HomePod + Chromecast speakers — playing the same audio, in sync, from one HDMI ARC return.

Why we ship hardware instead of riding existing streaming sticks

Obvious question: most households already have an Apple TV, Roku, Fire TV Stick, or Chromecast plugged into their HDMI. Why can't we ship as a tvOS / Roku / Fire TV app and skip the hardware entirely?

Honest answer: no streaming-stick app — Apple TV included — can capture the audio you'd want to fan out. The job for the living room is "take whatever the TV is playing (Netflix, Apple TV+, Disney+, broadcast, a console) and send it to every speaker in sync." A TV-platform app structurally cannot do that:

  • Apple TV / tvOS — this was originally our plan, and it doesn't work. tvOS has no system-audio / process-tap API, forbids apps from capturing other apps' audio, and locks down DRM/FairPlay content. A tvOS app could only fan out audio it plays itself — never the streaming services people actually watch. (For all-AirPlay-2 homes, the Apple TV already fans its own audio to every AP2 speaker natively, so a third-party app adds nothing.)
  • Roku — BrightScript channels are restricted to content streaming; no system-level audio routing APIs, no persistent background services, no sideloading. Closed.
  • Fire TV Stick — Fire OS allows sideloading but Amazon Appstore is hostile to non-Amazon multi-room solutions; Fire TV Multi-Room is their walled-garden play. Closed in practice.
  • Chromecast with Google TV — Android TV is more open but Google's Cast ecosystem is the intended path; not a host for cross-vendor orchestration. Tied to Cast.
  • Smart TVs with Tizen / WebOS / Roku OS — closed platforms generally.

That's exactly why Hub Stick / Hub Pro exist as a category. A hub on the TV's audio output (HDMI ARC / optical) captures the actual audio signal downstream of the sandbox and the DRM — the one place the content is finally just audio — then fans it out and syncs it across protocols. No TV-app sandbox can reach that signal; only hardware on the output can. See DECISIONS.md 2026-06-04 for the full reasoning behind dropping the tvOS-companion idea.

The price ladder + audience map

Five products may sound like SKU sprawl. The argument for it: each SKU addresses a distinct decision the household has already made. The Audio Bridge ($29) is for the user who's invested in their Sonos/WiiM/Bose soundbar and doesn't want to replace it. The Optical Hub ($79) is for the user who's open to replacing the soundbar entirely and wants TV-remote integration without HDMI. The Hub Pro ($249/$279) is for the user with a modern eARC TV who wants the full magic-moment experience. Each transition is a real upgrade with a real cost-justification.

The hardware sizing analysis (§4 below)

Same Pi Zero 2 W vs Pi CM4 vs CM5 analysis applies across all five SKUs. Hub Stick Mini + Audio Bridge + Hub Stick + Optical Hub all use Pi Zero 2 W (workload is light — just protocol fan-out, no HDMI processing). Hub Pro Dongle + Box use Pi CM4 (needs 5 GHz Wi-Fi + headroom for HDMI receiver chip). See §4 for the full table.

3HDMI-CEC remote passthrough + per-source profiles

The detail that makes the magic moment work. Standard mechanism (CEC), novel aggregation (mesh-wide).

What the TV remote actually does

HDMI-CEC carries a command called User Control Pressed with sub-codes for volume / mute / power / pause / etc. When you press a button on the TV remote, the TV doesn't change its own state — it sends the CEC command to whatever device has registered as the Audio System on the HDMI bus. Today that's your Sonos Beam (or HomePod, or AVR). Hub Pro takes that role and fans the command out.

TV remote actionCEC command Hub Pro receivesWhat Hub Pro does
Power on CEC Set Stream Path + ARC audio becomes active Auto-start session with saved per-TV profile · resolve speakers · ≤2 sec until audio plays everywhere
Volume up User Control Pressed (Volume Up) Bump every active sink by 5% of its slider value · preserves relative balance · iOS companion master-volume widget reflects new level
Volume down User Control Pressed (Volume Down) Inverse of above
Mute User Control Pressed (Mute) setVolume(0) on every sink · remember prior values · unmute restores
Power off ARC audio goes silent for >5 sec RTSP TEARDOWN to AP1 sinks · SOAP Stop to Sonos · everything goes idle cleanly

Per-source profiles

Each TV + input combination saves its own profile: which speakers, what EQ, default volume, per-sink offsets, surround behavior. CEC's Set Stream Path + the EDID Vendor Specific Data Block let us identify the TV (make / model). On first connection: "What TV is this?" picker if CEC EDID doesn't auto-resolve. After that — automatic.

A typical household profile set:

  • Living room Sony → A5 + A7 + Sonos Den, "movie night" EQ, all sinks at 70% master · saved offsets per the user's M5d tuning
  • Bedroom LG → just Bedroom Sonos, low-default volume (~15%), late-night EQ tilted toward dialogue clarity
  • Kitchen TCL → kitchen Chromecast, "news clarity" EQ for spoken-word content

Saved as JSON in the Hub Pro's eMMC, synced to the iOS companion. Same persistence layer as the Mac app's SessionState + SpeakerGroups — we extend, not rebuild.

Leverage existing soundbar — the Claude Skill as universal adapter

Real-world households don't have homogeneous audio gear. Your TV is LG OLED; your soundbar is Sonos Playbar; your other speakers are B&W AP1; the guest bedroom has a Bose Bluetooth speaker. The TV remote already controls the soundbar's volume — via the soundbar's IR receiver or HDMI-CEC integration. Why would we ask the user to replace that working chain?

Instead, SuperAudio software leverages the existing soundbar as the control layer. The planned (M6.5) Claude Skill — originally scoped for unsupported-speaker onboarding — would expand to cover any audio device in the household, including the soundbar acting as control surface. As designed, the Skill identifies the soundbar's make/model, probes for API/event endpoints, and writes a per-soundbar profile that the public superaudio-device-profiles repo contributes back to the community.

M5.5 schema gains a "control role" alongside "sink role"

The Device Profile substrate originally specced "sink role" only (a profile describes how to send audio to this device). Tonight's reveal expands the schema: profiles now describe two roles:

  • Sink role: receives audio (how to send, what codecs, timing tolerances, quirks). What we already specced.
  • Control role: provides volume / power / pause events for the mesh to propagate (which API to subscribe to, IR codes if learned, fallback to phone-app control).

A Sonos Playbar has both roles: it's a sink (receives audio) AND a control source (its volume events propagate to other speakers). A B&W A5 has only the sink role. A generic IR-only soundbar paired with an Audio Bridge has only the control role (the user keeps the soundbar for TV audio; the Audio Bridge captures audio for the rest of the mesh). The schema accommodates all three.

The pitch sharpens to: "Keep your soundbar. Keep your TV-remote habits. Our software (plus an optional dongle if needed) extends the experience to every other speaker you own — and our planned (M6.5) guided onboarding Skill helps figure out your specific gear's quirks in about 10 minutes of setup." Software adapts to your gear, not hardware-per-vendor.

4Hardware sizing — Pi Zero 2 W vs CM4 vs CM5

Honest engineering math, not marketing. The wrong chip kills either the price target or the customer experience.

Board SoC RAM Wi-Fi Board cost Total BOM $59 Hub Stick? $249 Hub Pro? 3 sinks 6 sinks
Pi Zero 2 W Quad A53 @ 1 GHz 512 MB 2.4 GHz only ~$15 $35–45 ✓ Fits ✗ Wi-Fi too weak ✓ ⚠ Tight
Pi 4 (2 GB) Quad A72 @ 1.5 GHz 2 GB 5 GHz Wi-Fi 5 ~$35 $55–70 ⚠ Blows by $5–10 ✓ Fits ✓ ✓
Pi 5 (2 GB) Quad A76 @ 2.4 GHz 2 GB 5 GHz Wi-Fi 5 ~$50 $80–95 ✗ ✓ Fits comfortably ✓ ✓
Pi CM4 (4 GB, Lite) Quad A72 @ 1.5 GHz 4 GB 5 GHz Wi-Fi 5 ~$25–30 module + ~$40 carrier $100–115 ✗ ✓ Sweet spot for $249 ✓ ✓
Pi CM5 (4 GB, Lite) Quad A76 @ 2.4 GHz 4 GB 5 GHz Wi-Fi 5 ~$70 module + ~$30 carrier $130–160 ✗ ⚠ Margin tight at $249 · works at $299 ✓ ✓

Why these picks

  • Pi Zero 2 W for Hub Stick — accepted-risk choice. CPU is enough for 3–4 sink households (the median). 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi 4 is the real constraint — for 6+ sink power-users on congested networks, audio glitches are likely. Hedge: M13.5 Hub Stick Plus at $99 on Pi 5 (2 GB) if real-world testing surfaces the glitch.
  • Pi CM4 for Hub Pro — sweet spot. Same A72 cores as Pi 4 (proven for our workload), 4 GB RAM (comfortable), 5 GHz Wi-Fi 5 (solves the Hub Stick wireless concern), purpose-built carrier-board form factor (clean industrial-mount design). BOM $100–115 leaves $130–150 margin at $249 retail.
  • Why not CM5: the A76 cores are nice but we don't actually need them for our workload (no heavy video processing, just audio fan-out). The $30–45 BOM premium burns margin we'd rather keep. Revisit if/when M14 v2 ships Atmos passthrough (more CPU-heavy).
  • Why not Adafruit's own hardware: Adafruit's Feather / ItsyBitsy / Metro lines are microcontroller-class (ESP32, RP2350, M0/M4). Wrong tier — we need a Linux-running SBC, not a microcontroller. Adafruit resells Pi which is fine; their own boards don't fit our workload.

5Design gaps — what we still need to figure out

Honest accounting of open questions. Tier 1 = must address before M14 build starts. Tier 4 = mostly settled, just documented.

Tier 1
Pre-build
eARC vs ARC capability scope at launch

eARC carries Atmos / DTS-X (compressed surround). ARC carries PCM stereo or Dolby Digital 5.1 only. Decision needed: target eARC (HDMI 2.1) for full Atmos passthrough, or ship M14 v1 with ARC stereo downmix and add Atmos in v2. Probably v1 = stereo downmix; v2 = surround split for engineering tractability.

Tier 1
Pre-build
HDMI passthrough vs ARC-only topology

Two ways Hub Pro can sit on HDMI: (a) ARC-only — Hub Pro plugs into TV's ARC return, doesn't touch the video signal. Simpler, cheaper, no HDCP complexity. (b) Full passthrough — Hub Pro between TV and source, handles HDCP, can override audio. More expensive ($350+ retail). Decision: ARC-only at $249. Passthrough is a future Hub Pro Plus SKU at $349 if customers ask.

Tier 1
Pre-build
HDCP handling

eARC carries encrypted streams. Realtek RTD2173 (and similar receiver chipsets) handle HDCP transparently — not our code, vendor stack. Need to confirm RTD2173 firmware supports HDCP 2.3 (required for Atmos over eARC on 2024+ TVs).

Tier 2
Post-launch
Surround audio split vs downmix

If TV outputs Atmos, do we (a) downmix to stereo for non-Atmos sinks (B&W A5, etc.), or (b) split — pass Atmos to capable sinks, stereo to others, in sync? Option (b) is the killer feature but real engineering. M14 v1 = (a); M14 v2 = (b).

Tier 2
Post-launch
Per-HDMI-input profiles

Modern TVs have 4–5 HDMI ports. Audio comes from different sources (cable box / Apple TV / console). Today our profile is "per TV"; "per HDMI input" needs CEC's Set Stream Path parsing. Real engineering, ship as v2 polish.

Tier 2
Post-launch
Voice-assistant conflicts

"Alexa, volume up" said in the room → only Sonos hears it locally → mesh goes out of sync. Two options: (a) accept as documented limit, (b) intercept Sonos's event subscription and propagate the change to other sinks. (b) is real engineering, post-launch polish.

Tier 3
Nice-to-have
TV USB-A port as power source for Hub Stick

Pi Zero 2 W draws ~150 mA idle, ~500 mA peak. Many TV USB-A ports do 500 mA+. Could eliminate the wall wart entirely — "plug into your TV's USB port, done." Real UX win. Needs power-management testing across TV brands to confirm.

Tier 3
Nice-to-have
Bluetooth A2DP receive on both products

Guest phones pair with Hub Pro/Stick → audio fans to mesh. Single-pairing limit (Bluetooth A2DP is one-at-a-time) but useful for guests, kids, anything without AirPlay/Cast support.

Tier 3
Nice-to-have
Boot time optimization

Hub Pro needs to be "ready" within 5 s of power-on so the TV-power-on auto-trigger doesn't miss the first second of audio. Default Pi Zero 2 W boot ~30 s; CM4 default ~15 s. Custom initramfs + direct-boot-to-daemon (no systemd full stack) gets both under 5 s. Real engineering effort.

Tier 4
Settled
Latency budget for CEC volume command → mesh

CEC command arrives (~10 ms) + parallel dispatch to N sinks (~50–100 ms RTT each, but parallel via TaskGroup) = ~100 ms worst case at 6 sinks. Below the 200 ms "instant" threshold. ✓ Fine.

Tier 4
Settled
Graceful TV-power-off teardown

ARC audio goes silent for 5 s → fire RTSP TEARDOWN to AP1 sinks + Sonos SOAP Stop + clean process exit. Reuses the existing applicationWillTerminate path from the Mac app. ✓ Fine.

6Manufacturing strategy — Latin America hybrid

The founder is a US/Colombia dual citizen with family in Mexico, Panama, and Dominican Republic. That's strategic optionality most indie hardware founders don't have. Use it.

Trade-agreement reality across the four countries

All four have zero-tariff agreements with the USA. That means the China-vs-elsewhere math becomes "is the BOM savings worth the IP risk + tariff exposure" — and at current US tariff posture (25–30% on Chinese consumer electronics, with discussion of higher), the answer is mostly no.

CountryTrade agreementTariff to USAElectronics ecosystemFounder access
Mexico USMCA 0% Strongest in LATAM. Guadalajara, Monterrey, Tijuana — Jabil, Flex, Sanmina, plus dozens of smaller specialists. Mature contract-mfg supply chain. Cousin holds dual citizenship · direct relationship
Colombia CTPA (2012) 0% Small for hardware mfg. Strong for software / firmware engineering talent — Medellín, Bogotá. Costs ~30–50% of comparable US engineering. Founder holds dual citizenship
Dominican Republic DR-CAFTA (2007) 0% Real electronics mfg in free trade zones (Santo Domingo, Punta Cana). Lower wages than Mexico. Smaller ecosystem but growing. Family in country · trust factor
Panama Panama TPA (2012) 0% Limited mfg. Colón Free Trade Zone is one of the world's largest — logistics + holding-company strength, not assembly. Family in country

Recommended function-by-country mapping

  • Software / firmware engineering → Colombia (Medellín or Bogotá) once we hire beyond the founder. Tech-talent arbitrage + founder's dual citizenship enables direct hiring. Audiophile-press story works.
  • Hardware design (PCB schematic, mechanical, firmware) → USA or Colombia. Contract PCB-layout firms in Bay Area / Austin (~$5–15 K) or a hired engineer in Medellín.
  • Component sourcing → Shenzhen via Alibaba. Most chips are made in Asia regardless. Sourcing components ≠ assembling them. Use verified suppliers (Seeed Studio for Pi-related, established chipmakers for the rest).
  • PCB assembly + final box assembly → Mexico (Guadalajara or Monterrey) via cousin's network. Best LATAM ecosystem + zero tariff + IP protection better than China + direct trust.
  • Lower-cost assembly hedge / backup → Dominican Republic for batch 2+ if Mexico costs creep. Family connection enables relationship.
  • Logistics / LATAM distribution hub → Panama (Colón FTZ) when we eventually distribute regionally. US market alone uses a US-based 3PL (ShipBob, Amazon FBA).
  • Certification (FCC / CE / IC) → US third-party labs, $8–14 K per market, one-time per design.

Marketing-story angle worth using

"Designed in Latin America, assembled in Mexico, engineered for the world." The David-vs-Goliath energy of an indie audio company with LATAM DNA + US tech expertise is genuinely compelling — and underused. Sonos hides its mfg origin; Bluesound markets Canadian roots; B&W markets British. SuperAudio markets regional dignity: not foreign-made, not American-corporate, but founded by someone with real cultural roots across the Americas. Audiophile press will eat this. Latin American market (post-M8 expansion) will respond to a brand of and from the region, not foreign brands condescending in.

Important honest caveats

  • This is months out. M13 ships after M5.5 + M6.5 + M7 + M8 + M9 + M10 + M11 + M12. Realistic timeline: 2027. Manufacturing outreach starts ~6 months before ship date. Plenty of time to vet factories.
  • Get a US international tax accountant + a lawyer familiar with US/LATAM hardware imports before committing to a holding-company structure. Probably $1–3 K of advice money but pays for itself if it surfaces tax benefits or unblocks a problem early.
  • MOQ realities. Most CMs in any of these countries want 250–500-unit minimums. At 100 units we'd be limited to small US CMs or specialty Chinese factories. Acceptable for a first run while we validate demand.
  • The factory pick happens after sample units + factory visits, not after this document. This is the framework, not the contract.

8Why this all matters

Where the Mac app gets to in M8 + the hub products from M13/M14 in combination.

The strategic point
The Mac app is the wedge. The hub products are the platform.

By the time Hub Pro ships, a household can have: Mac app on their MacBook (for direct-from-app audio), Hub Stick by the router (for headless audio orchestration), Hub Pro behind the TV (for the magic-moment "press TV power, audio everywhere" experience — the hub captures TV audio downstream of the sandbox + DRM, which no Apple TV / tvOS app can do). Every speaker the household owns — B&W from 2013, Sonos from 2018, HomePod from 2022, Chromecast from anytime — plays the same audio at the same time, tuned to each room, controlled by whatever interface is most convenient.

That's not "another multi-room product." That's universal audio orchestration for existing hardware — the layer above the fragmented current state, the thing that doesn't exist as a unified product anywhere on the market or on GitHub. The Mac app proves the hypothesis. The hub products turn it into a platform that scales beyond one household's geek.

Press TV power. Audio everywhere. That's the demo. Everything in this document exists to make that demo real and reliable.
