Product Case Study

Make every speaker in your house play the same song.
In sync. Tuned to the room. Across protocols.

Multi-room audio for households with mixed AirPlay 1, Sonos, and other "stranded" network audio gear that the modern AirPlay 2 and Sonos ecosystems quietly refuse to unify. The moat is auto-sync and room tuning that work across mixed protocols — what Sonos Trueplay does only for Sonos hardware, and what Dirac/Roon do only at $500+. Three SKUs, one codebase, $5–$80 entry points.

Engineering Notebook 2026-05-18 — 8 hours of debugging in one page

How we got AirPlay 1 and Sonos to play in sync (and how to make it painless for users)

A full day of testing distilled into one engineering narrative. The TL;DR: cross-protocol sync between AirPlay 1 and Sonos is fundamentally a measurement problem, not a tuning problem. Sonos's renderer buffer is variable, hidden, and unobservable via UPnP — the only way to actually sync is to listen.

The journey

We started thinking sync was about tuning a constant. Manual slider, pick the right number, save it. That works for one session — Sonos's this-session buffer state. Next session: same hardware, same network, different buffer state, audio is off by 600 ms. The manual slider isn't a solution, it's a workaround.

We tried UPnP-based auto-alignment. Sonos exposes AVTransport::GetPositionInfo which returns the playback position within the current stream. Subtract from wall-time elapsed since SOAP Play, and you get Sonos's apparent lag. Push that into AirPlay's broadcaster delay. Works in some sessions, off by 500+ ms in others. Across 8 sessions of empirical data, measured sonos_lag ranged from 3.59 sec to 4.62 sec for the same hardware. RelTime reports the renderer queue position, not the DAC. The hidden buffer between them varies session-to-session. Software-only fix is fundamentally limited.

Pivoted to mic-based cross-correlation. The plan was straightforward: record the Mac's mic while music plays, cross-correlate against the broadcaster's output, find each speaker's arrival lag. The Accelerate vDSP FFT engine worked perfectly on synthetic test signals — 7/7 chirps recovered within 1 ms. Then we tried it with real music. SNR locked at 20+ (statistically rock-solid) but the lag values were inconsistent with what the math said they should be.

Diagnosis: music has rhythmic and harmonic structure that creates spurious correlation peaks. Every beat, every chord interval, every repeated phrase produces a peak. The "true" peak at the speaker's actual lag is sometimes weaker than the spurious ones. Pro audio measurement tools (Smaart, REW, ARTA) all use chirps — linear sine sweeps with near-flat autocorrelation — for exactly this reason. Chirps give one clean unambiguous peak.

Replaced the ambient-music reference with a 1-second 200 Hz → 8 kHz sweep injected into the broadcaster. Within minutes we had the right lag values. Sequential per-speaker measurement (mute all but target, measure, repeat) handled the multi-speaker case cleanly. Three passes converged from 1000 ms residual to "playing in sync it's beautiful." That's the working algorithm.

Three structural insights that took 8 hours to find

  • Ambient music is a bad reference signal. Its rhythmic and harmonic structure creates spurious correlation peaks that beat the true peak. Pro audio uses chirps for this exact reason — and we should have started there.
  • Sonos's UPnP RelTime is unreliable. It reports the renderer queue position, not the DAC position. The hidden buffer between them varies ±600 ms session-to-session. Mic measurement bypasses this entirely.
  • The system converges iteratively. Each calibration pass reduces error by ~80%. One pass: 200–500 ms residual. Two: 50–100 ms. Three: sub-perceptual.

How to make it painless for users

The pain in today's testing was: you knew it was happening. The fix is invisibility — calibration runs silently as part of "I just want to play music." Three phases, escalating in polish:

  • Phase 1 (shipped 2026-05-18): auto-trigger after Sonos's SOAP Play succeeds. Calibration runs during Sonos's intrinsic ~30-sec settling ramp — the period where AP1↔Sonos sync is bad anyway. By the time Sonos hits steady-state, AP1 is already re-tuned to match. User clicks Play All, hears music start, sync locks in within ~15 sec, no further input. Toggleable in Preferences (default ON).
  • Phase 2 (planned): silent drift detection during playback. App heuristic flags when sync may have drifted (e.g., long playback gap on Sonos's HTTP stream) and schedules a brief recalibration between songs or during transitions where the chirp wouldn't be noticed.
  • Phase 3 (M11 polish): inaudible test signals. Ultrasonic sweep (18–22 kHz, near-inaudible to most adults) or pink noise masked under music. Continuous low-cost recalibration becomes possible — every few minutes, silent re-measurement. This is what Sonos's own Trueplay does internally (mic-based pulses inaudibly masked).
  • Phase 4 (M11 product surface): per-room profiles + iOS companion. Save calibration per environment ("Living Room", "Office"). iOS companion uses the phone's mic instead of Mac's — better positioning, can walk around the room. One-time setup ritual when adding a new speaker; lifetime of "just works" after.

The structural unlock is this: we now have a measurement primitive that no other competitor in our space exposes to users. Sonos Trueplay calibrates Sonos speakers for room acoustics. Apple Spatial Audio calibrates HomePod for the room. Neither calibrates cross-protocol sync between speakers they don't own. SuperAudio does — and it works on hardware Sonos and Apple won't touch (Playbar gen 1, AirPlay 1 receivers, AirPort Express). The marketing demo is two taps: "Press Play. Hear sync."

The framing that sells
SuperAudio isn't "another multi-room audio app." It's the software that resurrects AirPlay 1 — the only wireless protocol Apple ever shipped that's actually lossless — and connects it to every music source you use today.

Your 2014 B&W A5 can play Apple Music Lossless. Your $300 HomePod cannot. We make this fact actionable.

1The problem

Three vendors own modern multi-room audio. Each one assumes monoculture. None of them play the speakers most households actually own.

Multi-room audio in 2026 has three gatekeepers — Apple (AirPlay 2, requires every endpoint to speak AP2), Sonos (closed premium ecosystem), and Google (Chromecast, deprioritized since the Chromecast Audio discontinuation in 2019). Each assumes you bought all-in on their stack.

Most households didn't. A typical living room — median US household income (~$84K per 2024 ACS, released Sept 2025) all the way down to budget-conscious households — has:

  • A pair of B&W A5 or A7 speakers from 2014 — AirPlay 1 only, abandoned by AP2
  • A gen-1 Sonos Playbar from 2015 — Sonos UPnP only, no AirPlay support
  • An old Apple TV in a drawer — still works as an AP1 receiver, nobody's using it
  • A Bluetooth soundbar attached to a smart TV via HDMI ARC
  • A budget WiFi router from the ISP

None of the three ecosystems will play these together. Replacing this hardware with all-AP2 or all-Sonos costs $500–$2,000 per room.

The TV gap — the killer use case

When the TV is on, the audio is locked: TV → soundbar via HDMI ARC. The other speakers in the house sit silent. There is no consumer product today that says "turn on your TV, hear it everywhere, no phone or app required."

SuperAudio solves this three ways depending on the user's gear and budget — all share the same fan-out engine:

The stranded-hardware gap

Devices that were premium purchases 5–10 years ago still work perfectly as audio receivers. They just speak protocols the modern ecosystem has chosen to ignore. SuperAudio's bet is that this hardware is worth preserving and that the household will pay a small one-time fee to keep using it.

The room-tuning gap

Even when households do get audio to multiple speakers (e.g., via Sonos's all-Sonos solution), each speaker plays at a different distance from the listener, in a different acoustic space, with different driver characteristics. Without per-speaker delay and EQ tuning, the result sounds smeared or hollow. Sonos Trueplay solves this for Sonos hardware. Dirac and Roon solve it for $500+. Nothing solves it for mixed-protocol households at consumer prices.

Firmware posture (important context)

B&W abandoned firmware support for the A5/A7 years ago. Sonos still ships firmware updates for the Playbar gen 1. Either way, we don't depend on firmware updates from manufacturers. The protocols (RAOP / UPnP) are stable; receivers work; quirks get accommodated in our sender code, not in theirs. This insulates us from vendor-side obsolescence and means a 12-year-old B&W A5 is as supportable on launch day as it was the day it shipped.

A meaningful slice of households globally — anchored on the US median ($80K) but extending all the way down to budget-conscious owners of one nice speaker, and up to audiophile-curious upper brackets — will pay $19–$200 for a product that unifies their existing heterogeneous network audio hardware, auto-syncs them, tunes them to the room, and works with their TV, if the setup story is "scan a QR code" and the value story is "your old gear plus this works better than Sonos, and the TV one is the killer feature."

5The moat — auto-sync & tuning

Multi-protocol streaming is table stakes. Cross-protocol acoustic calibration is the durable differentiator. This is the section investors and reviewers care about most.

Auto-sync: four layers, each more sophisticated than the last

L1
Protocol latency baseline
Query each sink's reported buffer depth via RTSP/UPnP. Measure round-trip time. Compute initial per-sink delay. This is where most competing products stop.
±50 ms
L2
PTP-style time sync (host-time stamps)
Every audio chunk carries a presentation timestamp. Every sink schedules playback for that exact host-time. Solves the electrical sync problem at the protocol level. Already designed into the v1 architecture (CLAUDE.md).
±10 ms
L3
Acoustic calibration via microphone The moat
User stands at their listening spot, opens the SuperAudio iOS companion (or uses their Mac mic), app plays test tones through each speaker in turn, records the response, calculates the per-speaker delay from speaker-to-ear. Includes speed-of-sound through air (~3 ms per meter). This is what Sonos Trueplay does for Sonos only; ours works across every protocol.
±2 ms at listener
L4
Continuous drift correction
Listen for sync drift cues during normal playback, micro-adjust per-sink offsets in real-time. Probably overkill for v1; reserved for Phase 9+.
Phase 9+

Tuning: three categories, same calibration walk-through

Tune A · Trivial

Per-speaker volume matching
Measure SPL at the listening spot during the calibration sweep. Normalize so every speaker plays at the same perceived loudness. Free, automatic, included in the base Room Tuning addon.

Tune B · Manual

Per-speaker parametric EQ
3- or 5-band EQ per sink, user-adjustable in the menu UI. Useful for households who want to tame a bright Sonos Playbar or warm up a thin AP1 speaker.

Tune C · The moat

Room correction via mic sweep
Same calibration walk-through as L3 sync. FFT analysis of recorded sweeps yields per-speaker frequency response. Generate IIR filter coefficients per speaker. Apply DSP before encoding to each sink. This is what Dirac and Roon do at $500–$1,200; ours is $5 and works across mixed protocols.

Tune D · Optional

Bass management
Assign sub-80Hz to the speaker best suited for bass (usually the Sonos Playbar or a stereo subwoofer). Lighter speakers handle mids/highs. Reduces distortion, opens up the soundstage. Phase 8+ refinement.
The insight
One calibration walk-through powers both sync and tuning. The same 90-second microphone recording from the user's listening spot feeds both the time-domain analysis (per-speaker delay) and the frequency-domain analysis (per-speaker EQ correction).

Sonos Trueplay does this — but only for Sonos hardware, only on iPhone. Dirac Live does this — but at $499+, with pro-audio complexity, and requires Dirac-ready endpoints. SuperAudio's version is approachable, $5, and works across every protocol we support: AirPlay 1, AirPlay 2 (addon), Sonos, Chromecast (addon), Bluetooth (addon). That cross-protocol calibration does not exist anywhere else at any price.

12Open decisions

Don't need answers today; do before each milestone fires.

  • Room Tuning: $5 standalone or bundled with Mac? $5 standalone keeps unit economics clean; people who want sync without tuning aren't forced to pay. Leaning standalone.
  • iOS Companion: truly free, or freemium gate? Truly free serves the "amplify paid-product value" goal. Don't gate features users need to be happy.
  • Apple TV: $5 standalone, or free bundled with Mac? Leaning $5 standalone with occasional bundle promotions.
  • Build Hub Stick in-house or partner? Solo-developer cadence argues for partner (Adafruit-style consignment).
  • SD Card Kit before Hub Stick, or simultaneously? Leaning SD Card Kit first — lower ops, tests demand.
  • Open-source the Mac app post-launch? MIT-clean codebase already supports this; community-building lever.
  • Sales infrastructure: Paddle, Stripe, or Gumroad? Paddle handles EU VAT; Gumroad fastest to launch.
  • EU compliance posture (DSA / GDPR). Direct sale to EU needs VAT; Paddle covers it.
  • Room Tuning UI: walk-around vs single-spot? Sonos Trueplay's walk-around captures more spatial info but feels gimmicky. Single-spot is more typical. Test both during Phase 7.

13Why we won't pivot to hardware-first

It's tempting; we're explicitly not doing it.

  • The Mac app validates the entire stack technically. If RAOP, sync, Sonos, capture, or DSP don't work on a Mac with full debugging, they won't work on a headless Pi.
  • Hardware ops are a separate skill set. Fulfillment, SD-card flashing, RMAs, support — none of it scales until the software is proven.
  • Mac-app revenue funds Hub R&D. Indie cash flow has to come from somewhere.
  • Customers can DIY today. The SD Card Kit ships before the bundled Hub Stick, giving us a stepping stone.
  • The moat (Room Tuning) is software, not hardware. Building hardware first delays the differentiator that actually matters.

Phase 1 ships first. Room Tuning is the moat. Hardware is downstream.
